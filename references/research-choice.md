# Research Choice

Use this reference when the user must choose among research directions, topics, supervisors, environments, or plans.

## Decision Sequence

### 1. Define the decision

State the actual choice and deadline, desired outcome, non-negotiable constraints, acceptable risks, and what makes the choice reversible or irreversible. Do not evaluate an undefined choice.

### 2. Gather decision-bearing information

Separate available information from assumptions. Typical evidence includes recent work, demonstrated capabilities, resource access, time to first discriminating result, skill and career fit, dependencies, ethics constraints, and credible failure modes.

For a supervisor or environment, prefer observable evidence such as recent outputs, student outcomes, project fit, communication expectations, resource access, and authorship or data practices. Do not infer character from a few impressions.

### 3. Compare candidates

| Criterion | Why it matters | Evidence for A | Evidence for B | Confidence | Decisive gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Scores may summarize but never replace reasoning. State weights only when the user accepts them; show how the conclusion changes if a decisive weight changes.

### 4. Test the choice

For each leading candidate, ask what must be true, the cheapest check, the signal to continue, the signal to stop or switch, and the opportunity cost. Prefer a reversible probe before a large commitment.

## Output Contract

Produce the decision objective and constraints, candidate comparison, decisive assumptions and gaps, selection rationale or reason to defer, risks and stop conditions, and one next evidence-gathering action.

## Admission to Evidence and Claim

Advance when the direction and scope are explicit enough to state a testable question and identify what observations or sources could answer it. Otherwise reduce the most decisive uncertainty.
